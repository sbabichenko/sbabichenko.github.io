Quantitative researcher at Wells Fargo in Charlotte. I work on game theory, empirical Bayes, machine learning, and quantitative finance, and increasingly on high-dimensional probability and statistics. My PhD work at UCSB solved the problem of forecasting the forecasts of others, which economics has had to work around since Keynes. Here I share projects and writing about what’s been on my mind. Decision Mesh

Thousands of coins, each flipped a few times, and odds that change somewhere on the square. The estimator adds detail only where a false-discovery gate says the flips justify it, then stops on its own. It separates the odds from each coin's own effect and from the noise of the flips. Projects

Noise-State Calculus for Dynamic Games with Strategic Information

My dissertation. Each player keeps estimates of the primitive shocks instead of belief hierarchies, so beliefs, prices and policies are impulse responses and equilibrium is a fixed point in them. The information wedge prices changing an opponent's beliefs.

Forecasting and Manipulating the Forecasts of Others

Solo-authored, 2026. Finite-player games in which actions both move payoffs and reshape what opponents learn. In a two-player benchmark, the information wedge explains why pooling gains are mostly strategic, why optimal precision allocation can starve an inefficient player of information, and why signal precision changes the policy rules themselves.

Adaptive, continuous regression that refines the surface only where a false-discovery gate finds support, and decomposes the data into the surface, unit-level effects, and pure noise. The rectangular version splits one axis at a time, like a decision tree, so it scales to higher dimensions.
