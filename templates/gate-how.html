Decision Mesh · The gate · illustrated

How the gate decides

The decision mesh only cuts where the data justify a cut. This is how it decides. Every drawing below comes from one run of the estimator, in your browser, on coin flips drawn on this page.

Fitting…

Somewhere on this square the odds of a coin change. Six thousand sites each flip their own coin about twenty times, and each dot is one site’s share of heads.

Every site’s coin also carries a nudge of its own, so neighbours disagree by more than the flips explain. Half the sites are held out: the estimator never sees them, and they score the fit at the end.

The fit starts from a coarse mesh of right triangles with two things free: a level and a tilt. A triangle can only split through the midpoint of its longest edge, and a split that would leave a vertex hanging on a neighbour’s edge splits the neighbour too.

The fit starts from an 8 × 8 grid of rectangles with the corners and the two midlines free. A rectangle can be cut through either midline, so every cell offers both directions and the gate chooses. A vertex left hanging on a longer edge takes its height from that edge, and the surface stays continuous.

Every segment drawn here has a candidate vertex at its middle.

A vertex that isn’t free sits exactly at the average of its two parents, the ends of the segment it splits. A free vertex may depart from that average by its surplus.

Admitting a candidate means letting its surplus be something other than zero. A prior keeps each surplus near zero, more tightly the deeper the vertex: coarse corrections may be large, fine ones must be small unless the data insist.

A round offers every candidate at once and scores each against the current fit: would a surplus here help? The score adds up the residuals of the sites under the candidate’s tent.

Blue: the data want the surface higher there. Orange: lower. Bigger dots, stronger scores. Round 0 scored … candidates. Over the whole run, some more had too little data under them to be scored at all.

A raw score flatters. Three corrections make it honest. Each site’s residual is centred: shrinkage and the coarseness of counts give it a nonzero mean even where the surface is right.

The part of the tent that the existing coefficients could absorb is taken out, a first-order refit, so a candidate gets no credit for a slope its parents could make. The bias the prior’s shrinkage induces comes out too. What is left, divided by its standard deviation, is the candidate’s z.

One z means little alone, so the gate reads the whole round at once. If no candidate mattered, the scores would pile up in one bell curve: the null.

Lindsey’s method fits the density of the scores and reads the null off its middle, so a round that is shifted or spread out as a whole is judged against itself, not the textbook bell. Here the null is centred at … with spread … (the textbook: 0 and 1), and it puts … of the candidates down as noise.

The null gives each candidate a local false discovery rate: how likely it is to be noise, given its score. Best first, the gate takes the longest run whose average stays at or below 10%.

In round 0 that run is … long. A textbook cutoff, |z| > 1.96, would have taken ….

Admission is careful. The selected candidates go in one at a time, best first, each re-profiled against a surface the earlier admissions may already have moved, and each admitted only if it still improves the fit.

…

Then the next round, against the new fit. As the signal is used up, each round’s scores look more like noise, and its null closes in on the textbook one.

Three rounds in a row that admit nothing and the gate stops. This run took … rounds.

Two variances are refitted between rounds. The pool variance is the coins’ spread around the surface: as the mesh explains more, it falls toward the spread the coins really have.

The depth variance sets how far a surplus at each depth may stray. It is estimated by EM from the admitted surpluses: their squares plus their posterior variances, averaged by depth. Adding the posterior variance back is what keeps a depth from freezing at its floor.

What is left is a surface that is flat wherever the flips were silent, and cut finely only where they insisted.

The run: 6,000 sites on the unit square, each flipping a coin about 20 times (between 10 and 30), with odds from the picture chosen above plus a nudge per site (standard deviation 0.25 on the log-odds), fitted by triangular-decision-mesh or rectangular-decision-mesh compiled to WebAssembly. The scores, nulls, false discovery rates, admissions and variances drawn here are the engine’s own, from a trace of each round’s candidates; the trace is the builds’ one addition, and it changes nothing the fit computes. The fifth drawing is a one-dimensional sketch of the corrections, not the run.
