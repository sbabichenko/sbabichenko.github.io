Decision Mesh

A regression tree cuts the plane into boxes and predicts a constant in each, so its fit is a staircase. A decision mesh cuts the plane into pieces that share their corners and predicts across each piece from those corner heights, so its fit is continuous. Press play: three fits grow on the same noisy data, each given the same number of free parameters.

  • runs in your browser
  • triangles: a port of DecisionMesh.py, matching it to 1e-12
  • rectangles: the same rule on the geometry of rectangular-decision-mesh
  • a few milliseconds a step
How it grows, in four sentences

Triangles. Every edge carries a candidate vertex at its midpoint; activating it splits the one or two triangles on that edge through their opposite corners, and every active vertex can also have its height refitted. Rectangles. Every cell offers both of its cuts, so the refinement can follow the data's anisotropy instead of the diagonal a triangle happened to be born with; inside a cell the fit is bilinear, which carries the interaction of the two coordinates that a linear triangle cannot. A vertex left hanging in the middle of a coarser cell's edge takes that edge's endpoints as its parents, which makes the surface continuous with no constraints to store.

In both, each candidate knows the least-squares height it would take with every other height held fixed, and how much squared error that would save; each step takes the largest saving. The tree beside them is ordinary best-first CART. All three are grown until they have the same number of free parameters: one height per mesh vertex, one mean per tree leaf.

Error against the truth

Root-mean-square distance from the true surface over the square, against the number of free parameters; the dashed line is the noise σ. Both meshes beat the staircase almost everywhere once there are a hundred parameters to spend, and on these six surfaces the tree never finishes ahead, though it comes close where the truth jumps (the disk) or lines up with the axes (the ripples). Between the two geometries it goes the way the geometry argument says: the triangles lead where the structure runs diagonally (the saddle, the diagonal cliff), the rectangles where it lines up with an axis or spreads radially (the waves, the ripples, the disk, the hills), because a bisected triangle can only refine one way while a rectangle can be cut along either axis. Late in a run a thin piece holding a handful of points can take an extreme height: those are the jumps in the curves. A minimum of 3 points per piece is the default here (0 is the published triangular algorithm), and "refresh fits" refits the candidates around a vertex whose height has just moved, which the published algorithm leaves stale.

The triangular engine is a line-for-line JavaScript port of my Decision Mesh, matching the Python's squared error to 1e-12 over the first 400 steps. The rectangular one is written for this page: it is the geometry of rectangular-decision-mesh (dyadic bisection, two parents per vertex, bilinear cells) grown by the same greedy rule as the triangles, so the two panels differ only in their geometry. Data, cuts and pictures are all computed on this page.

None of the three knows when to stop: they grow until the page runs out of budget. The estimator I actually use chooses its cuts with an empirical-null false-discovery gate and stops by itself; it runs here too, compiled from its C++.
