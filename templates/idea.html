Dissertation · the idea, in pictures

From beliefs about beliefs to the noise-state

Why the dissertation writes everything as impulse responses. Every drawing below is computed on this page from one small problem: a state X pushed around by random shocks, watched by two players through their own noisy signals.

Two players watch the same thing through their own fog. Player 1 wants to know where the state is headed. That depends on what player 2 will do, which depends on what player 2 believes, including what 2 believes about player 1.

So player 1 forecasts player 2’s forecast, and the forecast of that, without end. Townsend called it forecasting the forecasts of others.

Step back. In a linear-quadratic-Gaussian game nothing moves on its own. Every price, belief and action moves because some primitive shock hit it: a surprise in demand, a trader’s news, the noise in a signal.

Call the shocks W. Above them, the state X they push around.

Hit the system with a single shock at time s and watch what follows. The trace it leaves is the impulse response L(t, s): how much of a shock at s still shows at t.

Linearity means responses add. Any quantity is its mean plus the responses to every shock so far, each scaled by the size of its shock.

{{ fx.linear.svg | safe }}

Kick by kick, one kernel rebuilds the whole path.

A player never sees W. From a noisy signal they estimate the whole path of shocks up to now. Each new observation revises their estimate of old shocks, too.

{{ fx.def.svg | safe }}

That estimate, a belief about the shocks rather than about the state, is the noise-state.

To forecast anything, a player runs the same kernel against their estimated shocks instead of the true ones. Conditioning changes the integrator and nothing else.

{{ fx.cond.svg | safe }}

Now the tower. Player 2’s forecast is itself a kernel against the shocks, so player 1’s forecast of it is that kernel run against player 1’s noise-state. A forecast of a forecast is a composition of kernels; on a grid of dates, a matrix product.

The regress closes, at any depth, without cutting the loop.

Strategies are kernels too, and the best response to kernels is a kernel. Equilibrium is a fixed point in kernels, found by iterating until they stop moving.

Those kernels are the impulse responses the explorer plots, and what the chapters compute.

The formal version is the section The noise-state of the introduction, and Chapter 1 builds it for the finite-horizon game.

The drawings use a discrete-time version: X follows Xk = 0.92 Xk−1 + wk, and player i sees Xk plus independent noise (standard deviation 1.2 for player 1, 2 for player 2). The estimates are exact Gaussian conditioning on all signals so far, and the page checks that each forecast built from the kernel equals the one computed directly. The last drawing iterates a filter’s kernel to its fixed point, standing in for a game’s.
