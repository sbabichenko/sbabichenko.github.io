No one knows much.

Noise-State Calculus for Dynamic Games with Strategic Information

Samuel Babichenko

No one person knows everything needed to make a pencil, yet pencils manage to exist, costing almost nothing.

Leonard Read, “I, Pencil”

Prices, for Hayek, were a telecommunications system. The logger never sees beyond their own corner; the prices that reach them carry what the rest of the world needs them to know.

Actions move the state of the system, the state generates observations, observations update beliefs. Beliefs complete the cycle by determining the actions that started it.

There is also a chord, where an action can set what others observe directly.

Every tractable approach severs the loop somewhere. The cuts fall on the privacy of beliefs, on what actions can teach, on the weight of one player, on the flow from observations to beliefs, or on time itself.

This dissertation builds a noise-state calculus that keeps the loop intact.

Each player keeps estimates of the primitive shocks instead of belief hierarchies about the endogenous state. Under perfect information, these estimates are the shocks themselves.

The information wedge is the shadow price of changing an opponent’s beliefs. It breaks the separation principle and vanishes when the loop is cut.
